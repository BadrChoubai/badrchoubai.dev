## Journey from Novice to Expert

**Summary:**

This chapter reveals to the reader the five levels of skill acquisition associated with the _Dreyfus Model_. These five levels are,

1. Novices
2. Advanced Beginner
3. Competent
4. Proficient
5. Experts

**And each are achieved in successive order on one's journey from novice to expert.**

### Skill Acquisition Levels

1. Novices
   1. Have little or no skill in a particular area.
   2. Primarily concerned with their ability to succeed; with little experience to guide them. Doesn't like to deal with uncertainty.
2. Advanced Beginner
   1. Can break away from a fixed ruleset, becomes more comfortable with uncertainty.
   2. They want information _fast,_ don't want to bogged down with lengthy theory. They can also begin to use advice in the correct context.
3. Competent
   1. Can start to develop conceptual models of a problem domain and work with those models effectively.
   2. They will seek out and troubleshoot problems on their own. Working from more deliberate planning and past experience. Although, if they fail to gain more experience, they'll still have trouble trying to determine which details to focus on when problem solving.
4. Proficient
   1. Can reflect on previous poor task performance, and revise their approach to better performance.
   2. Proficient practitioners need the big picture, they have the ability to learn from the experience of others and apply **maxims** (patterns (or principles)) which are applied in certain contexts.
5. Experts
   1. Experts are the primary sources of knowledge and information in any field. They are the ones who continually look for better methods and better ways of doing things.
   2. They work from **intuition,** not from reason. They know the difference between irrelevant details and the very important details, simply put, they are very good at targeted, focused pattern matching.

## This is your Brain

**Summary:**

The two parts of your brain, L-mode and R-mode thinking, why R-mode is just as important as L-mode and How to access it.

### Our Two Modes

Our brains have two modes, L-mode and R-mode. L-mode, is what gives us the power to work through the details of a problem and make shit happen, R-mode is critical for intuition, problem-solving and creativity.

#### R-mode

R-mode thought is asynchronous, it works in the background and is not always-on. When approaching a problem it is important to lead with R-mode and follow with L-mode.

## How to get there?

To cultivate R-mode and better integrate L-mode, there are a number of things you can do. You can write, go on walks, restructure a problem, or work with others. Another thing you can do is learn to make more metaphors.

### Morning Pages

Morning pages are self-writings that are three pages long, they require no planning. Wake up and write, do this before anything else—before your coffee, shower, or commute.

#### Rules

1. Write these _first_ thing in the morning
2. Three pages, on paper.
3. Do not censor what you write.
4. Do not skip a day.

### Go on Walks

Walk with no purpose, walk with no thoughts. As soon as you focus on a goal, L-mode processes will dominate.

### Change Your Viewpoint

Look at problems from the other way. An audio engineer makes the instruments sound as bad as possible, then makes them sound good. A *whack o*n the head forces you to change your perspective.

### Metaphors

Coming up with a good metaphor is hard, so don't use the first ones you come up with. Instead, make many metaphors, and once you've come up with one that fits the context your working in, you have to actually try it in practice.

## Debug Your Mind

**Summary:**

Our intuition is shaped by preconceived notions ("biases") that shape how we think about our worlds. We want to "debug" our systems, and this chapter introduces us to the problems and their solutions in order to accomplish this.

### Meet Your Cognitive Biases

1. **Need for Closure →** We are not comfortable with doubt and uncertainty.
2. **Exposure Effect →** we tend to prefer things that are familiar.
3. **Hawthorne Effect →** People have a tendency to revert back to old habits/behaviors once the novelty of new ones wear off.

### We Just Can't Predict

Humans like to try to predict the future by extrapolating it from past events. We assume that events form a more or less stable, linear progression, with easily defined cause and effect. They don't.
