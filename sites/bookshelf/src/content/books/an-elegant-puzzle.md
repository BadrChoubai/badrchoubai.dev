## Chapter 3: Tools

### 3.2 Product Management—Exploration, Selection, Validation

Most engineering organizations separate engineering and product leadership into distinct roles.

#### Iterative Process of Product Development

- **Problem Discovery -** uncovering possible problems to work on
  - The first phase of a planning cycle is exploring the different problems that you could pick to solve,
  - Themes for _populating_ a problem space
    - Users' Pain
      - What are the problems that your users face?
    - Users' Purpose
      - What motivates the users of your system?
    - Competitive Advantages
      - By understanding the areas you're exceptionally strong in, you can identify opportunities that you're better positioned to fill than other companies.
    - Compounding Leverage
      - What are the composable blocks you have in place today that you could start building today that would compound into major technical or product leverage?
        - An infrastructure example might be moving a failing piece of technology to a new standard.

- **Problem Selection -** filtering those problems done to a viable subset.
  - Once you've identified enough possible problems, the next challenge is to narrow down to a specific problem portfolio.
  - **Surviving the round.** What do you need to survive this current round? This might be the revenue that the product will need to generate to avoid getting cancelled, adoption, etc.
  - **Consider different time frames.** When folks disagree about which problems to work on, it is most frequently rooted in different assumptions about the correct time frame to optimize for.
  - **Industry trends.** Where do you think the industry is moving, and what work will allow you to leverage those trends, or at least avoid having to redo the work in the future.
  - **Experiments to learn.** What could you learn now that would make problem selection in the future much easier?
- **Solution Validation -** ensuring that your approach to solving those problems works.
  - Once you've narrowed down the problem you want to solve, it's easy to want to execute immediately, it's well worth it to take the risk out of your approach with an explicit solution validation phase.
  - **Find reference users.** Can you find users who are willing to be the first users for the solution? If you can't you should be skeptical whether what you're building is worthwhile.
  - **Find the path more quickly traveled.** The most expensive way to validate a solution is to build it in its entirety. Try to find the cheapest way to validate.
  - **Justify switching costs.** What will the costs of switching be for users who move to your solution?

### 3.15 Communities of Learning

**Be a facilitator, not a lecturer.** Folks want to learn from each other more than they want to learn from a single presenter. Step back and facilitate.

**Small breakout groups.** Giving folks time to discuss in small groups allows them to learn a bit about the topic in a small, safe place. It also gives everyone an opportunity to be part of the discussion, which is a lot more engaging then listening to others for an hour.

#### Meeting Structure

- Check In
  - Share: your name, your team, one sentence on top priority.
- Opportunities
  - a lot of learning is top-down
  - We can learn a lot from peers. Learning as a habit, learning in a community .
- Discussion
  - Break into groups of 4-5
  - Where do you learn today? Who do you learn from?
  - regroup in ten minutes
- Regroup
  - Summary from each group.
